Starting from my undergraduate work in a biophysics super-resolution microscopy lab. I have had a great passion in playing right on the fine line of product development and research. When walking this edge, I believe one gets the greatest benefit of each world and a perspective that enables better targeted development in both sectors. For example, my first project in the biophysics lab was to build an on-stage microscope incubator that maintained consistent temperature and CO2 concentration with the goal of enabling mammalian cell analysis. Since mammalian cells require very specific environments and super-resolution techniques take quite a bit of time, this hardware was a clear requirement to enable more foundational scientific investigations in closer to human cell models. After building this on-stage enclosure and Arduino based PID control software for a solenoid valve and resistive element, our lab could now perform mammalian cell studies. With this new capability, my next project in the lab transitioned from hardware/product development to scientific investigation where I developed a nano-thermometry technique for measuring cellular temperature gradients with a spatial resolution of ~1um and a thermometry accuracy of +-0.5C. This project utilized the incubator that I had made to keep mammalian cells alive for hours at a time on the microscope, stain the mammalian cells with BODiPY fluorescent dye, measure the stokes to anti-stokes fluorescent ratios, adject ambient temperature, measure stokes to anti-stokes ratios again, and step through this cycle multiple times to map the fluorescent rations to temperatures. This characterized temperature vs fluorescent ratio was then used to assess the temperature of cellular mitochondria to determine the validity of a popular publication claiming that mitochondria could heat up to as much as 50C.
